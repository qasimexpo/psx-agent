For a lot of Pakistani retail investors, dividends are the point. Fertiliser, power and oil and gas companies on the exchange have historically paid out a large share of profits, and a portfolio built around them is closer to a rental property than a lottery ticket. This guide covers how a dividend gets from the boardroom to your bank account, how to compare payouts, and where to see what is coming.

## How a dividend is announced

A company's board declares a dividend alongside its results, and the announcement goes to the exchange, which publishes it. The announcement gives three things:

- the **amount**, written as a percentage of face value, which for most PSX companies is Rs 10 per share;
- whether it is **interim** (paid during the financial year) or **final** (declared with the annual accounts);
- the **book closure** dates, the period during which the shareholder register is frozen to determine who gets paid.

So "60 percent final cash dividend, book closure 21 to 28 October" means Rs 6 per share for everyone on the register when it closes. The [ticker suffix guide](/guides/psx-ticker-suffixes-xd-xb-xr) explains what happens to the share price and symbol around those dates.

## Working out the yield

The yield is the annual cash dividend divided by the price you pay. Two traps:

**Percentages are of face value, not price.** A 60 percent dividend on a Rs 10 face-value share is Rs 6. If the share trades at Rs 225, the yield on that payout is 6 ÷ 225, about 2.7 percent, not 60 percent. Companies with a different face value change the arithmetic, so check the face value in the announcement if a number looks too good.

**Add up the year.** Many companies pay two, three or four interim dividends plus a final. The yield that matters is the total over twelve months. A single 25 percent interim looks modest until you notice there were three of them.

A rough sense of scale: at the time of writing, established dividend payers on the exchange have offered trailing yields in the high single digits to low teens in percent, with bank deposit rates as the usual comparison. Yields that far exceed the sector are often a falling share price rather than a generous company.

## What is deducted before it arrives

Dividends are paid net of **withholding tax**, deducted at source by the company at the rate set in the current Finance Act. The rate is materially lower for taxpayers on the Active Taxpayers List than for those who are not, which is one of the more practical reasons to file. The rate changes with the federal budget, so check the Federal Board of Revenue's current schedule rather than a figure in an article; this one included.

The net amount is credited to the bank account or CDC sub-account linked to your brokerage account, usually within a few weeks of the book closure ending. If you hold physical certificates, which is rare now, payment is by dividend warrant.

For investors who keep their holdings Shariah compliant, some scholars advise **purification**: donating the share of a dividend that corresponds to the company's non-compliant income, which the index screen allows up to a small percentage of revenue. The [compliance guide](/guides/how-to-check-if-a-psx-stock-is-halal) has more on what the screen permits.

## Bonus shares and rights are not dividends

Two other entitlements look like dividends on an announcement and are not.

A **bonus issue** gives you more shares for free, in proportion to what you hold. Your stake in the company is unchanged; you have more pieces of the same pie, and the price per piece falls to match. Companies issue bonus shares to grow their paid-up capital without spending cash. Nothing arrives in your bank account.

A **rights issue** offers you the chance to buy new shares, usually below the market price, in proportion to your holding. It is the company raising money from you, not paying it to you. If you do not take up the rights, your stake is diluted; the rights themselves can normally be sold on the exchange during the offer period. A rights issue is worth reading carefully: it can be a sign of an expanding company or a stretched one.

## Where to find upcoming payouts

Three places on this site.

**The company page.** Under the Performance card, **Upcoming** lists any announced payout with its book closure dates, and any scheduled meeting. The [stock page guide](/guides/how-to-read-a-psx-stock-page) shows where it sits. A line such as "payout 60% (F) (D), book closure 2026-10-21" reads as a 60 percent final (F) cash dividend (D) with book closure from 21 October.

**The home page.** The [dividends and corporate actions](/#events) section collects the next few weeks of book closures across the market, with a Shariah badge on each compliant company, next to the calendar of shareholder meetings.

**The daily brief.** The [closing brief](/brief) mentions notable announcements of the day, and the [guide to reading it](/guides/how-to-read-the-daily-brief) explains where.

For the authoritative version, the exchange's own announcement is on the PSX website, and your broker will show book closure dates against each holding.

## A short checklist before buying for the dividend

1. **Total the last twelve months of payouts** and divide by today's price. That is the real yield.
2. **Check the ex-date.** Buying the day before it does not capture anything; the price adjusts.
3. **Look at the payout against profit.** A company paying out more than it earns is borrowing to pay you, which does not last.
4. **Check compliance** if it matters to you. Several of the highest-yielding names on the exchange are conventional banks. The [directory](/stocks) filter shows which companies are Shariah compliant.
5. **Check the trend and volume** on the company page. A high yield on a stock in a steady downtrend is often the market's opinion of the next dividend.

None of that is advice about any particular company. It is the arithmetic that stops a dividend from being a surprise.
