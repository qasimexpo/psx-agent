Sooner or later a company you hold will announce a right issue, and a few weeks later a new line appears in your broker statement: the same company, with an R after the name, and a number of shares you never bought. Many investors ignore it and let it lapse. Sometimes that is fine, and sometimes it quietly costs them money. This guide explains what is going on and how to decide.

## What a right issue is

A right issue is a way for a listed company to raise money from the people who already own it. Instead of selling new shares to outsiders, the company offers them to existing shareholders first, in proportion to what each one holds. Because you are being asked for fresh money, the offer price is usually set below the market price, which is the main reason to pay attention.

Companies raise rights for different reasons: to fund an expansion, to pay down expensive debt, to meet a regulatory capital requirement (common for banks and insurers), or because they are in trouble and need cash. The reason matters more than the discount, and it is always stated in the announcement and the offer document.

## Reading the announcement

A right announcement gives you three numbers.

- **The ratio.** Written as a percentage, for example a 25% right issue. That means 25 new shares for every 100 you hold, or one for every four.
- **The price.** Usually quoted per share, often at face value (Rs 10) or at face value plus a premium. Compare it with the current market price to see the discount.
- **The book closure dates.** As with dividends, the register is frozen for a few days. If you hold the share before the ex-rights date, the entitlement is yours.

On the ex-rights date the symbol starts trading with an XR suffix, which is explained in our [ticker suffix guide](/guides/psx-ticker-suffixes-xd-xb-xr). The entitlements then trade separately for a limited period.

## Why the price drops on the ex-rights date

Before the ex-date, a share carries the right to buy new shares cheaply. After it, it doesn't. The market adjusts the price for that, and the adjustment has a standard formula called the theoretical ex-rights price (TERP):

TERP = (existing shares × price before ex-date + new shares × right price) ÷ (existing shares + new shares)

A worked example. You hold 100 shares trading at Rs 60. The company announces a 25% right at Rs 40.

- Value of your 100 shares: 100 × 60 = Rs 6,000
- Cost of the 25 right shares: 25 × 40 = Rs 1,000
- Total value after subscribing: Rs 7,000 for 125 shares
- TERP: 7,000 ÷ 125 = Rs 56

So the share should open around Rs 56 on the ex-date, not Rs 60. Nobody lost Rs 4 a share. The value moved into the right entitlement, which is worth roughly the gap between the TERP and the offer price: 56 − 40 = Rs 16 each, or Rs 400 for your 25 rights.

That last number is the one people forget. If you do nothing, those Rs 400 disappear.

## Your three choices

When the entitlement arrives you can do one of three things.

**1. Subscribe.** Pay the right price for all the new shares by the deadline. Your share of the company stays the same, and you keep the full value. Choose this if you still believe in the company and the reason it is raising money, and you have the cash.

**2. Sell the entitlement.** For a short window the rights trade on the exchange under their own symbol, usually the ticker followed by R. Selling them gives you roughly the Rs 16 per right from the example, without putting in new money. Your stake in the company gets smaller, but you are compensated for it. This is the sensible choice if you like the company but don't want to add more cash.

**3. Let it lapse.** If you neither subscribe nor sell, the rights expire worthless after the deadline. The price drop from the ex-date has already happened, so you simply lose the value of the entitlement. This is almost never the right choice unless the amount is too small to bother with.

A partial version of options 1 and 2 also works: sell some rights and use the money to subscribe to the rest.

## How subscribing works in practice

The details depend on your broker and the company's share registrar, but the shape is the same everywhere:

- the entitlement is credited to your CDC account, where you can see it in your broker's app or CDC statement;
- the company publishes a subscription period with a last date for payment;
- payment goes through your broker or directly to the bank accounts listed in the offer document, using the forms or electronic facility the offer describes;
- the new shares are credited to your account after the company completes the allotment, which can take a few weeks.

Ask your broker for the exact steps a week before the deadline, not on the last day. Late payments are not accepted.

## Questions to ask before you pay

A discount on its own is not a reason to subscribe. Before putting in more money, read the offer document and check:

- **What is the money for?** Growth projects and debt reduction are different stories from covering losses.
- **Is a sponsor or major shareholder subscribing in full?** If the people who control the company are not putting in their share, ask why.
- **Is the issue underwritten?** Underwriting means someone has committed to buy any rights left unsubscribed, which gives some comfort that the company will raise the money it needs.
- **What happens to earnings per share?** More shares means profits are split more ways. If the new money doesn't earn enough, EPS falls. Our guide to [reading EPS and the P/E ratio](/guides/how-to-read-eps-and-pe-ratio-on-the-psx) shows how to check.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the price drop as a loss.** It is an adjustment. Your rights hold the difference.
- **Missing the trading window.** The rights trade for a limited period, which ends before the subscription deadline. If you want to sell, sell early.
- **Not keeping cash ready.** Right issues are often announced with a few weeks' notice. If you hold companies that tend to raise capital, keep some cash available.
- **Ignoring small entitlements.** Even a few hundred rupees per holding adds up across a portfolio, and every year.

## The short version

A right issue is the company asking its owners for more money at a discount. The share price falls on the ex-rights date by roughly the value of the right, and that value sits in your entitlement. Subscribe if you want to keep your full stake and trust the reason for the raise, sell the rights if you don't want to add cash, and avoid letting them lapse.
