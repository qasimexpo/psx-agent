Two numbers show up on almost every stock page, broker report and WhatsApp tip: EPS and P/E. They are the quickest way to see what a company earns and what the market is paying for those earnings. They are also the easiest numbers to misread. This guide explains both, with examples in rupees, and the questions to ask before you call a stock cheap.

## Earnings per share (EPS)

EPS is the company's profit after tax divided by the number of shares in issue.

EPS = profit after tax ÷ number of shares

If a company earned Rs 2 billion after tax last year and has 200 million shares, its EPS is Rs 10. Every share is entitled to Rs 10 of that year's profit, whether it is paid out as a dividend or kept in the business.

PSX companies report results every quarter, and each set of results shows EPS for the period. That creates the first trap: **quarterly EPS is not annual EPS.** A company reporting Rs 3 for the quarter is on course for roughly Rs 12 a year only if every quarter looks the same, which in seasonal businesses such as fertiliser, cement and textiles it often doesn't.

The fairer number is **trailing twelve-month EPS**: the sum of the last four quarters. It uses real results, not a forecast, and it smooths out the seasons.

### Basic and diluted EPS

Results often show two EPS figures. Basic EPS uses the shares in issue today. Diluted EPS also counts shares that could be created later, from convertible instruments or employee options. If the two are close, ignore the difference. If diluted is much lower, more shares are coming and each one will get a smaller slice.

## The price-to-earnings ratio (P/E)

The P/E ratio compares the share price with EPS.

P/E = share price ÷ EPS

A share at Rs 120 with trailing EPS of Rs 10 has a P/E of 12. In plain terms, you are paying Rs 12 for every Rs 1 of annual profit. Flip it over and you get the **earnings yield**: 1 ÷ 12 is about 8.3%. That is a useful way to compare a stock with a bank deposit or a government bond, as long as you remember profit is not the same as cash in your hand.

A lower P/E means you pay less for each rupee of profit. That is the whole attraction of "low P/E" stocks. Whether it is a bargain depends on why it is low.

## Compare within a sector, not across the market

Different businesses deserve different multiples. Banks, fertiliser and oil and gas companies on the PSX have often traded at lower P/Es than technology or consumer companies, because their profits depend heavily on interest rates, gas prices or government policy, and investors want to be paid for that risk. Companies expected to grow quickly trade at higher P/Es because buyers are paying for future profit, not just this year's.

So a P/E of 5 is not automatically cheap and 25 is not automatically expensive. The useful comparison is:

- the company's P/E against **others in the same sector**;
- the company's P/E against **its own history**;
- the P/E against **how fast earnings are growing**.

## Five traps to check before you call a stock cheap

**1. One-off gains.** A company that sold land, received a large insurance claim or booked an exchange gain can show a jump in profit that will not repeat. EPS spikes, P/E collapses and the stock looks cheap. Read the results announcement: one-off items are usually listed separately.

**2. Cyclical peaks.** Cement, steel, chemicals and commodity businesses earn the most at the top of their cycle. That is exactly when their P/E looks lowest. Buying a cyclical stock on a low P/E at the peak is one of the most common ways to lose money.

**3. Losses and tiny profits.** If EPS is negative, P/E means nothing. If EPS is very small, P/E becomes huge and swings wildly. For these companies, look at revenue, debt and cash flow instead.

**4. New shares.** Right issues and bonus issues add shares. If the share count goes up and profit doesn't keep pace, EPS falls even though the business hasn't changed. Our guide to [right shares](/guides/right-shares-on-the-psx) explains how that works.

**5. Old prices or old earnings.** A P/E calculated with today's price and last year's annual EPS can be badly out of date. Use the latest four quarters, and recalculate after each results season.

## A worked comparison

Two companies in the same sector:

| | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Share price | Rs 90 | Rs 150 |
| Trailing EPS | Rs 15 | Rs 12 |
| P/E | 6.0 | 12.5 |
| Earnings growth (3 years) | flat | about 18% a year |
| One-off gain in last year | Rs 5 per share | none |

On the headline number, A looks twice as cheap. Strip out the one-off gain and A's recurring EPS is Rs 10, so its real P/E is 9. Add that its earnings haven't grown, while B's have grown steadily, and the gap narrows a lot. B may still be the more expensive share, but the comparison is no longer obvious. That is what checking the five traps does.

## Where P/E fits in your process

P/E is a first filter, not a verdict. Use it to find shares worth a closer look, then check debt, cash flow, dividend history and what the company says about the next year. Dividend investors should also look at the payout, covered in our [dividends guide](/guides/dividends-on-the-psx).

When you review your holdings, note the P/E you paid and the P/E today. If the multiple has doubled without earnings growing, the market has become more optimistic, and that is worth knowing. Our [portfolio audit guide](/guides/how-to-audit-your-psx-portfolio) walks through the rest of that check.

## The short version

EPS is profit per share. P/E is the price you pay for each rupee of that profit. Use trailing twelve-month EPS, compare P/Es within a sector, and before calling a stock cheap, check for one-off gains, cyclical peaks, losses, new shares and stale numbers.
