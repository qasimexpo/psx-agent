# Pakistan's retail investors are asking WhatsApp whether a stock is Shariah compliant. The exchange already publishes the answer.

**Target outlets:** ProPakistani (Business section), Profit by Pakistan Today (Opinion), Business Recorder (Op-Ed), Mettis Global (Perspectives).
**Byline:** Qasim Riaz, founder of SmartSarmaya, a free research site for the Pakistan Stock Exchange.
**Length:** ~950 words. **Status:** draft, distinct from anything on smartsarmaya.com. Do not also post on the site.

---

Roshan Digital Accounts, brokerage apps that open in an afternoon, and a market that has spent two years making headlines have brought a new kind of investor to the Pakistan Stock Exchange: someone with a salary, a phone, and no broker relationship to speak of. Many of them want their holdings to be Shariah compliant. Almost none of them know how that is decided.

Watch the WhatsApp groups where these investors gather and the pattern is the same every day. Someone asks whether a cement company, a fertiliser company or a bank is Shariah compliant. Someone else answers in one word. Nobody says where the answer came from, and the group moves on. Portfolios get built this way, one confident reply at a time.

The strange part is that the authoritative answer is public, free and updated on a schedule. The exchange publishes it.

## The list nobody reads

Since 2008 the PSX has calculated the KMI-30, a Shariah-compliant index built with Al Meezan Investments and screened under the supervision of a Shariah advisory board. Most investors have heard of it. Far fewer know that the exchange also publishes the **KMI All Shares Islamic Index**: not thirty companies, but every listed company that passes the same screen, currently around three hundred names.

That list is the definition of "Shariah compliant" that Pakistan's Islamic mutual funds use. It is reviewed twice a year against audited accounts. And it answers the WhatsApp question directly: if a company is a constituent, it passed; if it is not, it did not.

The screen itself is not mysterious. A company's core business must be permissible, which rules out conventional banking, insurance, tobacco and alcohol outright. Then a set of financial ratios apply: interest-bearing debt below a threshold share of assets, non-compliant investments and income below their thresholds, enough illiquid assets that the share represents a real business rather than a pool of cash. Fail any one and the company is out until the next review.

This is why the answers change. A cement producer that borrowed heavily for a new line can drop out of the index despite selling nothing but cement. A textile exporter can fail on the interest its cash pile earns. Compliance is a property of the balance sheet on the review date, not a permanent label attached to a sector.

## Why the confusion persists

Three reasons, in my experience.

The first is that the KSE-100, the number on every news bulletin, is a size index. It contains the hundred largest companies by free float, and a large share of its weight sits in conventional banks. "It's in the KSE-100" tells an investor a company is big. It is routinely heard as "it's fine".

The second is that the KMI-30 is mistaken for the whole Shariah-compliant universe. It is thirty large companies. Investors who only consider those names are choosing from roughly the top tenth of what is actually available.

The third is simply that the All Shares list is a spreadsheet on an exchange website, and nobody opens spreadsheets on their phone. The information exists; the interface does not.

## What a better interface looks like

The fix is not complicated. Any company page, on any site or app, could state the answer in its first sentence and cite the source: "Yes, this company is a constituent of the KMI All Shares Islamic Index." A portfolio tool could total the share of an investor's money that sits in constituents. A filter could hide everything else.

I built a free site that does this, and I mention it not as a plug but as evidence that it takes an evening, not a budget. The exchange's data portal is public. The index constituents are public. The only decision a builder has to make is to treat the exchange's list as the single source of truth and refuse to second-guess it.

That last point matters more now than it did a year ago, because the next wave of investing tools will be built on language models, and a language model asked whether a company is Shariah compliant will answer fluently and be wrong often enough to do harm. The right design is to never ask it. Filter the universe to the exchange's list first, let the model rank and explain what remains, and throw away any name it produces that was not on the list it was given. The compliance decision stays with the exchange and the scholars who advise its methodology, where it belongs.

## What investors can do today

Three habits, none of which need a tool.

Check the list, not the index headline. The PSX publishes KMI All Shares constituents; a company is either on it or not.

Re-check after each review. Membership changes semi-annually, and a holding that was compliant last year may not be now.

Treat the screen as a screen. It is the standard Pakistan's Islamic funds use, and it is a set of ratio tests, not a personal ruling. For a holding that matters to you religiously, the list tells you the exchange's view; a qualified scholar tells you yours.

The retail investor who opened an account this year has better access to Pakistan's market than any generation before. The information to invest according to their principles has been public the whole time. It is only the WhatsApp group that was in the way.
